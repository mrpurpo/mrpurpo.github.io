*Originally written in Spanish for a sustainability course at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile.*

In recent years, frontier Artificial Intelligence (AI), primarily LLMs such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and others, has gained massive and unprecedented relevance, moving from a distant promise of science fiction to an everyday technology that is currently redefining the global economic and labor fabric. With this in mind, world powers saw this powerful tool as an opportunity for progress, investing an enormous amount of capital to build ever-larger models, meaning models fed with more data and parameters to give more accurate, more "intelligent" answers. This AI fever rests upon a massive expansion of real-world infrastructure: data centers, power plants, and next-generation semiconductors, transforming what was initially a software and algorithms race into a geopolitical and material battle for strategic resources. Now, this expansion of AI is leaving an alarming environmental footprint, from energy expenditures comparable to entire countries and water consumption equivalent to more than a million people to unprecedented carbon emissions, and it has barely been three and a half years since the public launch of ChatGPT.

Against this backdrop, the growth interests of major Big Tech companies (such as Nvidia, Google, and OpenAI) collide with the lack of regulation in most countries and the real biophysical impact on local communities. Furthermore, people are already taking a stance on AI, as it not only affects the environment, but has also disrupted the labor, social, and economic spheres. Faced with this crisis, my thesis is that, in order to neutralize the environmental impact of AI, we must rely on sustainable or degrowth policies, since corporate promises and our current trajectory in general will only lead to further destruction of ecosystems. This is because corporations today prioritize capital accumulation over sustainability and human well-being. Therefore, resolving the ecological problem of AI requires imposing strict environmental and democratic limits through a slowdown or a definitive technological degrowth.

## Planetary Boundaries and the Physical Ceiling of AI

We are already reaching the growth ceiling of AI because planetary boundaries have already been crossed, and it is only a matter of time before ecosystem disruptions directly affect all global infrastructure, including data centers and electrical grids. In this way, the tech industry is expanding while knowing that in a few years the resources and space needed to sustain it will no longer exist.

We know that global compute capacity has tripled every year since 2022 due to massive private investment in hardware infrastructure. However, the total energy capacity of these centers reached 29.6 GW by the end of 2025, matching the peak demand of New York State or entire European nations. At the same time, carbon dioxide emissions from training skyrocketed from 0.01 tons in *AlexNet* (2012) to 72,816 tons in *Grok 4* (2025), equivalent to the lifecycle of more than a thousand cars, to which we must add daily inference water consumption that drains water resources equivalent to the annual needs of 1.2 million people (Sajadieh et al., 2026). These data demonstrate that the biophysical cost of brute-force scaling is already critical and will continue to worsen under current commercial dynamics (Sajadieh et al., 2026).

This collision between digital infrastructure and biophysical limits ceases to be a theoretical projection when we observe territorial conflicts in the real world. Industry pressure to increase computing power has led corporations to contest spaces that are vital for conservation and local communities. This dispute is evident in examples such as Lake Tahoe in Nevada, United States, where the electricity provider, *NV Energy*, announced that by May 2027 it will stop supplying power to the area's nearly 50,000 residents in order to redirect service to nearby data centers; residents must find a new provider because there is no body regulating this supply chain (Gioino, 2026). A closer example is Quilicura, a municipality in Chile that currently hosts five data centers, including one owned by Google, which sought to compensate for its installation with the *Bosque Urbano Quilicura* initiative, a project that today stands abandoned and in very poor condition. Quilicura is one of the municipalities hardest hit by drought in Chile, and the installation of data centers only worsens the situation, which is why local communities and socio-environmental organizations fight day after day against the data centers already built and those yet to be built (Ortiz, 2025). These examples of physical space colonization show that the drive to build ever more massive models generates a destructive environmental footprint whose consequences are felt primarily by ecosystems and local populations.

## Algorithmic Efficiency Over Brute-Force Scaling

Simply increasing the size of models is not only inefficient, but also comes with all kinds of risks, whereas improvements in model architecture would generate a much healthier impact and multiple benefits for both the industry and the general population. In this regard, Bender et al. (2021) warn that model hypertrophy not only increases environmental harm, but also renders training data inscrutable, opaque, and biased, perpetuating social harms without the affected communities receiving any benefit. From this, it follows that scientific research must urgently prioritize energy efficiency over raw size, which would generate long-term benefits and avoid risks.

A strong counterargument is presented in "The Bitter Lesson" (Sutton, 2019), an essay widely accepted by the scientific community. It argues that increasing compute capacity will outperform any attempt to improve these models through human ingenuity. To illustrate this, it cites the example of *DeepBlue* in chess, the computer that defeated Kasparov in 1997; this computational feat was not the result of applying human understanding of chess to a system, but of maximizing the computer's processing capacity to analyze up to 200 million moves per second. Another example is *AlphaGo*, which defeated Lee Sedol in 2016, not through a deep human-crafted design, but by taking a simple system (in this case, machine learning) and scaling it as much as possible. Sutton thus concludes that slowing down AI scaling would only hold back the exponential growth of AI, which, for all we know, could even provide better solutions to climate change and other pressing problems today.

Nevertheless, this notion of "progress" is an unsustainable fallacy. Exponential growth that depletes the drinking water of entire communities to sustain inference processes is not technological evolution; it is planned ecological obsolescence, that is, the conscious decision by corporations to mass-deploy a technology knowing beforehand that it will destroy the planet's resources in the short term, forcing us to pay the environmental cost of a system born with an ecological expiration date. Slowing down brute-force scaling does not destroy the potential of AI; rather, it forces science to seek algorithmic efficiency. Contemporary alternatives demonstrate that architectural optimization can surpass the attempt to increase compute indefinitely. Models built on methodologies such as Mixture of Experts, implemented in DeepSpeed-MoE, or the mathematical design of DeepSeek-V3 show qualitative leaps in the performance-to-resource ratio. In fact, DeepSeek-V3 generated just 597 tons of carbon dioxide during its development (Liu et al., 2024), demonstrating that frontier progress does not always depend on material capacity. Moreover, redirecting the focus toward algorithmic design decentralizes the ecosystem: capital stops paying for the electricity consumption of infrastructure and is instead invested in human capital. This not only expands the researcher market, but also provides the deceleration margin needed to consolidate disciplines dedicated to improving model safety and evaluating their alignment with social well-being.

## Governing AI as a Knowledge Commons

Implementing these public policies would represent an enormous step forward for the common good, constituting a milestone in regulating companies that operate at the margins of social well-being. Transitioning toward degrowth policies and making this technology *open source* (making the model's code publicly available) is the way to make the functioning of models transparent and thereby promote understanding and critical evaluation by the interested community. According to Frischmann et al. (2014), the governance of knowledge commons offers a community-based institutional framework that enables the shared creation and exchange of science and data, solving resource production problems without getting trapped in the logic of the market or centralized state control. Similarly, this approach reminds us that alternatives for the collective management of natural and cultural resources exist beyond economic privatization (Instituto para el Desarrollo Sustentable UC, 2024).

From a geopolitical perspective, one might counterargue that this solution is impractical due to the technological and arms race between the United States and China, where neither power will cede strategic advantage, postponing any regulation. Likewise, leaders of the closed AI industry, such as Dario Amodei (CEO of Anthropic), argue that forcing models to be *open source* would grant access to dangerous tools. His position points out that releasing code to the public removes the corporate ability to monitor misuse, revoke access, or apply remote security patches, making it easier for malicious actors to strip away ethical filters to design cyberattacks or mass disinformation campaigns.

However, the current secretive and centralized race among Big Tech companies (which monopolize hardware and infrastructure) already represents the most insecure scenario possible. Corporate secrecy prevents environmental auditing, accountability, and safety verification. The governance of knowledge commons does not promote a "ruleless anarchy," but rather establishes community-based institutional structures of mutual control and shared access rules (Frischmann et al., 2014). In this way, the paralysis of nation-states in passing regulatory laws for this technology could be neutralized, avoiding both ecological tragedies across territories and technological safety risks through collective and transparent oversight.

## Rethinking Progress and the Role of the Engineer

In synthesis, the quantitative evidence on the global environmental impact of AI reveals the absolute impossibility of sustaining the paradigm of infinite growth. The biophysical cost in gigawatts, water footprint, and tons of carbon generated by frontier AI demonstrates that the current model destroys the material foundations of the territories that sustain it. Consequently, limiting compute infrastructure and transitioning in a regulated manner toward a knowledge commons model constitutes a fundamental ecological necessity.

As a Mathematical Engineering student at UC, I am deeply interested in the theoretical and technical side of how Artificial Intelligence works. I am interested in solving problems related to this technology, such as the *Alignment Problem*, which consists of ensuring that AI aligns with our human values and goals, preventing the model from ignoring our well-being and the material limits of our planet. However, this personal interest comes into conflict with the current global situation, where AI, while useful, seems to be doing more harm than good. I therefore believe it is necessary to first solve these problems born from the AI boom before moving on to the next step, which is improving and regulating the model itself. Even so, I strongly doubt that growth will stop or slow down anytime soon, above all because of the human pride and ambition that have brought us this far. For this reason, I consider implementing the proposed solution (imposing limits on AI through institutions) to be very difficult, though not impossible. Our current role as engineers, as the ones who design these systems, should not be to keep optimizing models blindly, but to take into account the crisis we find ourselves in, take a step back, and reconsider the ethics of what we are building: asking ourselves *Does it truly help people?*, *Does it respect the biosphere?*, among other ethical questions that are sometimes hard to ask amidst so much mathematics and work to be done.

In summary, frontier Artificial Intelligence systems are unsustainable, and fixing this requires deep changes in their political regulation. Looking further ahead, these changes can be projected not only onto politics and economics, but onto a broader mindset. I hope that little by little, as the narrative of the environmental crisis unfolds, we can build the collective mentality that allows us to solve this enormous problem. I therefore hold great hope for the future; although uncertain, I trust that humanity will be able to move forward, and I will do everything in my power to help make that happen.
